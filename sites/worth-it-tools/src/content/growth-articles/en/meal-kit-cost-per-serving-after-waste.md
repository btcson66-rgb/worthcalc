# Meal Kit Cost Per Serving After Waste: Count Uneaten Ingredients

> **Quick answer:** Divide the meal-kit cash cost plus delivery and extras by servings actually eaten, not servings printed on the recipe card.

## Collect the inputs that change the result

Record box price, delivery, taxes, add-ons, servings promised, servings eaten, and replacement groceries. Count discarded ingredients or unused portions as cost, not as a discount.

## Formula and worked example

effective cost per eaten serving = (box + delivery + taxes + extras + replacement ingredients) ÷ servings eaten. A $48 box plus $6 delivery promises four servings. If two are eaten and $4 of missing staples are bought, the effective cost is $29 per eaten serving, not $13.50.

## Use a range instead of a promise

USDA consumer guidance encourages meal planning and reducing uneaten food. Test a normal week, a busy week, and a week with one missed meal before comparing the kit with grocery shopping. Run low-use, realistic-use, and high-use cases. If the result changes, show the range and the assumption that changes it. Do not count a theoretical serving as value. If leftovers are eaten later, record that later use; if they are discarded, keep the waste visible.

Keep a short usage log for a month or cycle. Separate unavoidable costs from optional add-ons, and record why a planned use did not happen. That record makes the denominator auditable instead of optimistic.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can everyone use the same threshold?

No. Prices, usage, timing, and terms differ. Replace the example with your records and keep low, base, and high cases visible.

### What should I check first?

Are meal kits cheaper than groceries? Compare eaten servings and replacement purchases for your household; the printed serving count is not enough. Recalculate when terms, price, or usage change.
