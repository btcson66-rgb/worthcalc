## The 2026 figures

| Limit | 2026 |
| --- | ---: |
| 401(k)/403(b)/457 elective deferral | **$24,500** |
| Catch-up, age 50+ | $8,000 |
| Catch-up, ages 60–63 only | **$11,250** |
| Maximum deferral, ages 60–63 | **$35,750** |
| IRA contribution | $7,500 |
| IRA catch-up | $1,100 |

The elective deferral rose $1,000 from 2025. That is the headline, and it is the least interesting number on the list.

## The number that expires

The catch-up at ages 60 through 63 is **$11,250 against the ordinary $8,000** — $3,250 more, for four calendar years, after which it reverts.

    extra sheltered space = $3,250 × 4 years = $13,000

Nothing else in the table behaves this way. Miss a year of ordinary contributions and you can contribute more later, subject to that year's limit. Miss a year of the 60-to-63 catch-up and it is gone: there is no carry-forward, and you cannot use it at 59 or at 64.

For someone in their late fifties planning the last stretch before retirement, that window is worth planning *around* — front-loading other savings so cash flow is available in exactly those four years.

## What a marginal dollar of contribution is worth

    tax deferred this year = contribution × marginal rate
    future value          = contribution × (1 + return)^years

On the $1,000 of new headroom for 2026, at a 22% marginal rate:

| | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Tax removed from this year's bill | **$220** |
| The $1,000 after 20 years at 7% | **$3,870** (pre-tax on withdrawal) |

The $220 is certain and arrives in April. The $3,870 is an assumption, and it is taxed when it comes out. Only the first is a reason to act *this* year; the second is a reason to have started years ago.

## Where this sits in the order of operations

Contributing to the cap is not automatically the best use of the money. The ranking that falls out of the arithmetic:

1. **Enough to capture the full employer match.** An immediate 50–100% return beats everything below.
2. **Debt above roughly 8%**, highest rate first. A 24% credit card outruns any expected market return.
3. **Then the remaining headroom**, including the 60-to-63 catch-up while it exists.

Deferral is also not the same as exemption. A traditional contribution moves tax from today to withdrawal, so the real benefit is the *difference* between your rate now and your rate then — not the full 22%.

## What would reverse the conclusion

- **A lower rate now than later.** If you expect to withdraw in a higher bracket than you are in today, Roth treatment is worth more than the deduction, and the same limit shelters more after-tax money.
- **Plan rules below the statutory limit.** Some plans cap deferrals as a percentage of pay or fail testing, so the IRS limit is a ceiling, not an entitlement.
- **Cash flow you need.** Money in a 401(k) is hard to reach before the qualifying age. An emergency that goes onto a card at 24% costs more than the deferral saved.
