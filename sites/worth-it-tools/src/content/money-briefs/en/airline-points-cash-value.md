## The only valuation that means anything

Every travel site publishes a cents-per-point table. They are estimates, and the organisations publishing them earn money when you apply for the card. Your own number is easy to compute and is the only one that describes what happened to your money.

    cents per point = (cash price you would have paid − award taxes and fees) ÷ points redeemed × 100

Two words carry all the weight: **would have**. The comparison is against a ticket you would genuinely have bought with money.

## The arithmetic

Worked example — an economy award seat costing 40,000 points plus $56 in taxes and carrier fees, on a route where you would have bought the $520 cash fare:

| Line | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Cash price you would have paid | $520 |
| Less award taxes and fees | −$56 |
| **Value obtained** | **$464** |
| Points spent | 40,000 |
| **Cents per point** | **1.16¢** |

Now the same redemption valued against a $2,900 fare you would never have bought:

    ($2,900 − $56) ÷ 40,000 × 100 = 7.11 cents per point

The second number is the one screenshotted and shared. It is also fictional, because no $2,900 was ever going to leave your account. You did not save $2,844; you consumed 40,000 points for a seat worth $464 to you.

## Points versus plain cashback

Once you have a real cents-per-point figure, the card comparison is one division:

    break-even earn rate = cashback rate ÷ point value

Against a flat 2% cashback card and a 1.16-cent point:

    2 ÷ 1.16 = 1.72 points per dollar

| Card earns | Effective return at 1.16¢ | Beats 2% cashback? |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| 1 point per $1 | 1.16% | No |
| 2 points per $1 | 2.32% | Yes |
| 3 points per $1 (category) | 3.48% | Yes, in that category only |

A general-spending card earning one point per dollar loses to flat cashback at this valuation, and it loses every month, quietly.

## What points are, structurally

A point is an unsecured liability of a company that can reprice it unilaterally and has a commercial reason to do so. It pays no interest, it can expire, and its purchasing power has historically fallen. Cashback is money.

That is not an argument against points — a 3x category bonus redeemed at 1.5 cents genuinely beats 2% cash. It is an argument for computing your own number and holding balances for months rather than years.

## What would reverse the conclusion

- **A category bonus you actually spend in.** 3 to 5 points per dollar on groceries, dining or travel comfortably clears the break-even, but only on that spending.
- **Transfer partners and premium cabins.** Where a transfer genuinely buys a seat you *would* have bought in cash, the cents-per-point figure can be several times higher. The test remains the counterfactual.
- **An annual fee.** Subtract it first. A $95 fee is about 8,200 points of redeemed value before the card has beaten a free 2% alternative.
- **Award fees on your route.** Some carriers levy surcharges that make a short-haul award worse than the cash fare. Always run the subtraction before booking, not after.
