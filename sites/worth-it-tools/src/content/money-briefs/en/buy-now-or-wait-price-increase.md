## Two costs, pointing opposite ways

Waiting to buy costs whatever the price does while you wait. Buying before you have the money costs interest. The decision is a comparison of those two numbers, and most of the advice on it only ever counts one.

    cost of waiting      = price × expected price rise over the wait
    cost of buying early = price × APR × (months ÷ 12)
    break-even increase  = APR × (months ÷ 12)

## The arithmetic

Worked example — a $1,200 purchase, a six-month wait to save the cash, general price rises of about 3% a year, and a credit card at 23.79%.

| Path | Cost |
| --- | ---: |
| Wait six months, pay cash | $1,200 × 3% × 0.5 = **$18** |
| Buy now, carry on the card six months | $1,200 × 23.79% × 0.5 = **$143** |
| **Difference** | **$125 in favour of waiting** |

At general inflation, waiting six months is nearly free and financing is not. The price would have to move about eight times faster than the index for buying early on credit to break even.

## The threshold that makes the decision

The break-even does not depend on the price at all — it cancels out:

    break-even increase = APR × (months ÷ 12)

| Financing | 3-month wait | 6-month wait | 12-month wait |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Cash (no financing) | 0% | 0% | 0% |
| 0% promotional | 0% | 0% | 0% |
| 7% personal loan | 1.8% | 3.5% | 7% |
| 23.79% credit card | 5.9% | **11.9%** | 23.8% |

So: **if you have the cash, buy ahead of any genuine increase** — the cost of waiting is positive and the cost of buying is zero. If you would carry it on a card, an announced increase has to clear roughly 12% over six months before buying early wins.

## Why announcements are not forecasts

A seller announcing a price rise is doing two things: informing you, and moving demand forward. Announced increases are sometimes delayed, sometimes smaller than trailed, and are frequently followed within months by discounting that lands below the old price.

That does not make them fake. It means an announced increase should be treated as a probable event with a magnitude, not as a deadline — and it should be multiplied by your own estimate of whether it happens before being compared to a financing cost that is certain.

## What would reverse the conclusion

- **A specific, large, credible increase.** A supplier-wide change, a tariff with a stated date, or a model-year transition can exceed the threshold comfortably. The larger and more specific the number, the more the arithmetic points to buying.
- **A purchase that pays for itself.** If the item reduces a bill or earns income, each month of waiting has its own cost that belongs on the waiting side. An appliance saving $30 a month is $180 over six months, which dwarfs both figures above.
- **A category that gets cheaper.** Electronics usually fall in price and improve in specification. There the cost of waiting is negative and the arithmetic points firmly the other way.
- **0% promotional financing you will actually clear.** With no interest accruing, the cost of buying early is zero and any genuine increase makes buying now correct — provided the balance is gone before the regular rate resumes.
