## The comparison the rule of thumb gets wrong

"Never spend more on a repair than the car is worth" is the most widely repeated rule here, and it compares two things that are not alternatives. The car's trade-in value is what you would *receive*. The alternative to repairing is what you would *pay*. Those differ by thousands.

The comparison that works puts both paths in the same unit — dollars per month of transport:

    repair path      = (repair cost + expected further repairs) ÷ months gained
    replacement path = (price − trade-in + interest) ÷ months of ownership

## Worked

A car with a $2,800 transmission repair. Trade-in value as-is: $2,100. The replacement is a $24,000 used car at 8.4% over 60 months, kept 5 years.

| | Repair | Replace |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Cash outlay | $2,800 | $24,000 |
| Trade-in received | — | −$2,100 |
| Interest over the term | — | $5,000 |
| Expected further repairs over the window | $1,000 | $600 |
| **Total** | **$3,800** | **$27,500** |
| Months it buys | 24 | 60 |
| **Cost per month** | **$158** | **$458** |

Add the lines the comparison usually drops. Insurance on the newer car runs about $40 a month higher, and registration on a higher-value vehicle adds roughly $8:

| | Repair | Replace |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Cost per month, base | $158 | $458 |
| Extra insurance | — | +$40 |
| Extra registration | — | +$8 |
| **Cost per month, all in** | **$158** | **$506** |

The repair costs about a third of the replacement per month of transport, on a car the rule of thumb says to scrap because the bill exceeds the $2,100 trade-in.

## Where the repair path actually loses

It loses on the **months gained** term, and only there. Hold everything else and vary it:

| Months the repair buys | Repair cost per month |
| ---: | ---: |
| 36 | $106 |
| 24 | $158 |
| 12 | $317 |
| 6 | $633 |
| 3 | $1,267 |

Below about six months the repair stops competing, and that is the real decision rule: **repair until the repairs stop buying time.** A car needing a major repair every four months has not become too expensive to fix, it has become a car whose repairs buy nothing.

The corollary is that the estimate of months gained is the whole answer, and it should be the shortest credible one — governed by the *next* thing to fail, not the thing you are fixing now.

## What would reverse the conclusion

- **Negative equity on the existing loan.** If you owe $9,000 on a car worth $6,000, the $3,000 shortfall rolls into the replacement loan. That lands in the replacement numerator and usually settles the comparison before anything else is considered.
- **Repairs clustering.** Two major failures inside a year means the months-gained estimate for the third one should be short. The pattern is the signal, not any single invoice.
- **The car is unsafe or strands you.** Rust on structural members, a failing safety system, a fault that leaves you stationary on a motorway — none of these are in the formula, and all of them can outrank it.
- **A genuinely cheap replacement.** The comparison assumes a $24,000 car. Against a $9,000 replacement kept four years at the same rate, the replacement path is about $182 a month, and a repair buying under 21 months no longer wins.
