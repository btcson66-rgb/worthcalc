## The shape of the payout

Almost every insurance product on this site has a payout that rises with the size of the loss. Dental is the exception: it rises, then stops.

    net benefit = min(covered costs × coverage rate, annual maximum) − premium − deductible

Worked with a common structure — $35/month premium, $50 deductible, $1,500 annual maximum, 100/80/50 coverage tiers:

| Dental spending in the year | What the plan pays | Premium + deductible | Net |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $0 | $0 | $470 | **−$470** |
| $250 (two cleanings, exam, x-rays) | $250 | $470 | **−$220** |
| $900 (above, plus two fillings) | $770 | $470 | **+$300** |
| $2,200 (above, plus a crown) | $1,420 | $470 | **+$950** |
| $4,000 (crown, root canal, extraction) | $1,500 (capped) | $470 | **+$1,030** |
| $9,000 (implant work) | $1,500 (capped) | $470 | **+$1,030** |

Read the last two rows. Spending more than doubles, and the benefit does not move at all. The plan's usefulness peaks somewhere around $2,500 of treatment and is flat forever after.

That is the opposite of what insurance is for. You buy cover because you cannot absorb the bad year — and the bad year is exactly where this product stops responding.

## Where the cap bites

The annual maximum is the entire story, and it bites at a level that is no longer high. A single crown is commonly quoted in the $1,000–$1,800 range, and an implant with abutment and crown well above that. So:

- **One crown** in a year roughly exhausts the cap on its own.
- **A crown plus a root canal** exceeds it, and everything past the maximum is paid in full by you, at the plan's negotiated rate rather than its coverage rate.
- **The 50% tier on major work** means you reach the cap on $3,000 of treatment, not $1,500.

That last point is the one people miss. At 50% coverage, $1,500 of benefit requires $3,000 of billed major work — so the cap is reached later than expected, but the co-payment on the way there is half of everything.

## The honest framing

Run the plan over five years, with one expensive year in the middle:

| Year | Spending | Plan pays | Premium + deductible |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 1 | $250 | $250 | $470 |
| 2 | $250 | $250 | $470 |
| 3 | $4,000 | $1,500 | $470 |
| 4 | $250 | $250 | $470 |
| 5 | $600 | $530 | $470 |
| **Total** | **$5,350** | **$2,780** | **$2,350** |

Five-year net: **+$430**, on $5,350 of dental work including one genuinely bad year. That is a real gain, and it is also a rounding error against the size of the underlying spend — which is the accurate way to describe this product. It smooths routine care. It does not protect you.

**Self-funding comparison.** The same $2,350 of premiums, paid from an HSA or FSA instead at a 30% combined tax rate, has an effective cost of $1,645 and covers any amount of treatment with no cap, no tier and no waiting period. Over that five-year window the two paths land within a few hundred dollars of each other — and the pre-tax route has no ceiling on the bad year.

## What would reverse the conclusion

- **An employer pays most of the premium.** This changes everything on this page. If your payroll deduction is $8 a month rather than $35, the break-even falls to roughly one cleaning and the plan is straightforwardly worth taking.
- **Known, expensive, scheduled work — and the patience to wait out the waiting period.** Orthodontics in particular is often written with its own lifetime maximum, and enrolling a year ahead of treatment is one of the few cases where timing the purchase works.
- **A household of four.** Preventive utilisation scales with people. Four sets of cleanings and exams push a plan past break-even on routine care alone, before any restorative work.
- **No access to pre-tax dollars.** The self-funding comparison above leans on the HSA or FSA discount. Without one, paying cash costs the full sticker price and the plan's relative position improves.
