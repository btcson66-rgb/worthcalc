## The headline number and the real one

Group long-term disability is usually quoted as replacing **60% of base salary**. That sounds like a 40% cut. It is generally worse, for a reason that has nothing to do with the policy's generosity.

When the **employer** pays the premium, the benefit is taxable. When the **employee** pays it from after-tax income, it generally is not.

Worked example — a $70,000 salary, 60% group cover, employer-paid:

| | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Net income from working (after ~22% tax) | **$54,600** |
| Gross benefit (60% of $70,000) | $42,000 |
| Benefit after tax at 21% | **$33,180** |
| **Annual shortfall** | **$21,420** |

The effective replacement is about **61% of net income**, not 60% of gross — and the household's expenses did not fall by 39%.

## The premium question people get backwards

Where an employer offers the choice of paying the LTD premium pre-tax or having it treated as taxable income to you, taking the **taxable** treatment is usually right.

The premium is small and the benefit is large, so paying tax on the premium to make the benefit tax-free is an excellent trade — you are choosing to be taxed on a few hundred dollars instead of on tens of thousands, in the year you can least afford it.

## The four things that shrink a group policy

A stated 60% can become much less:

1. **A monthly cap.** Common, and it binds first for higher earners. A cap of $5,000 a month is 60% only up to a salary of $100,000.
2. **The definition of disability.** "Own occupation" pays if you cannot do *your* job. "Any occupation" pays only if you cannot do *any* job you are reasonably suited to. The second is far harder to claim on.
3. **What counts as earnings.** Many group policies cover base salary only. For someone earning half their income in bonus or commission, 60% of base can be 30% of actual income.
4. **The waiting period.** Typically 90 to 180 days with no benefit. That is what the emergency fund is for, and it is the part most people have not sized.

## How to size the gap

    annual shortfall = net working income − net benefit actually payable

Then reduce it by what a partner's income and savings can genuinely absorb, and insure what is left. Sizing on the replacement percentage rather than on the shortfall is how households end up buying cover they do not need on top of cover that does not reach.

## What would reverse the conclusion

- **An employee-paid premium.** A tax-free benefit closes most of the gap in the example above without buying anything extra.
- **A partner's income that covers essentials.** The shortfall is a household number, not an individual one.
- **A short horizon to retirement.** The value of income protection is roughly the income it protects, so it falls as the remaining working years fall.
- **Income that is mostly not base salary.** This makes the group policy *worse*, not better, and is the strongest case for supplemental individual cover.
