## The arithmetic nobody runs

A pre-sale renovation has four terms, and published "return on investment" figures include only two of them:

    net effect = value added − project cost − financing cost − extra carrying cost

Worked example. A $40,000 kitchen, expected to recoup 60%, financed on a home equity line at 9% for the eight months between starting work and closing:

| Term | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Value added at resale | +$24,000 |
| Project cost | −$40,000 |
| Financing, $40,000 at 9% for 8 months | −$2,400 |
| Extra carrying cost, 2 months of delay at $2,600 | −$5,200 |
| **Net effect on proceeds** | **−$23,600** |

The headline recoup rate said you would lose $16,000. Financing and delay took it to $23,600. Both of those terms are real, both are routinely omitted, and both get worse the longer the project runs — which is the direction projects run.

## Why the recoup rate falls as spending rises

There is a pattern in the aggregate figures that survives across markets and price bands: **cheap projects recoup better than expensive ones.** Not because expensive work is done badly, but because of what the two are actually buying.

| Project | Typical spend | What it changes |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Paint, declutter, deep clean | $1,500–$3,000 | Whether the listing photographs work |
| Landscaping and front door | $2,000–$5,000 | Whether buyers get out of the car |
| Minor repairs, hardware, lighting | $1,000–$4,000 | Whether the house reads as maintained |
| Bathroom remodel | $15,000–$30,000 | One room, against buyer taste |
| Kitchen remodel | $30,000–$80,000 | One room, against buyer taste |

The first three change whether the house competes. The last two change a room, to a specification the buyer did not choose — which is why a buyer will not pay the full cost of a kitchen they would have finished differently.

## The one case that genuinely pays

Some houses do not sell at any price a seller will accept, because a defect puts them outside what most buyers can finance or insure. A roof that fails inspection, a non-functioning heating system, an unsafe panel, active water intrusion. Fixing those is not a renovation decision:

    value of the fix = the sale happening at all

That is an unbounded return against a bounded cost, and it is the only category where the money is clearly well spent. The test is whether the defect blocks a sale, not whether it improves one.

## What would reverse the conclusion

- **A market where the renovated standard is the baseline.** Where every comparable sale has a finished kitchen, an unfinished one is not a discount, it is a disqualification — and the project moves into the "unlocks the sale" category above.
- **You do the work yourself, competently.** The recoup rate is against total cost, and labour is typically half of it. A $40,000 kitchen done for $18,000 in materials recoups 133% on that basis — but only if the finish stands up to an appraisal and an inspection.
- **A long remaining tenure.** If you will live with it for ten years, resale value is the wrong test. The right one is what you would pay for ten years of a better kitchen, which is a consumption question with a perfectly respectable answer.
- **A structural change with a code-recognised value.** Adding a legal bedroom, or converting unfinished space into permitted living area, changes the property's comparables rather than its finish. That is the one renovation category that can reprice a house rather than decorate it.
