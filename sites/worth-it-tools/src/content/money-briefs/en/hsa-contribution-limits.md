## The 2026 and 2027 official figures

| | 2026 | 2027 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| HSA contribution limit — self-only | **$4,400** | **$4,500** |
| HSA contribution limit — family | **$8,750** | **$9,000** |
| Additional contribution — age 55+ | **+$1,000** | **+$1,000** |
| HDHP minimum deductible — self-only | **$1,700** | **$1,750** |
| HDHP minimum deductible — family | **$3,400** | **$3,500** |

Watch for stale figures: **$4,300 and $8,550 are the 2025 limits, not the 2026 limits.** IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-19 sets the 2026 limits at $4,400 and $8,750. Revenue Procedure 2026-24 then raises the 2027 limits to $4,500 and $9,000.

The age-55 additional contribution remains $1,000 under the IRS HSA contribution rule. Your personal limit can still be lower if you are not HSA-eligible for the full year, if employer contributions use part of the limit, or once Medicare enrollment begins.

## The payroll point that actually matters

"Contribute through payroll" is too broad. IRS Publication 15 draws a specific line:

- an ordinary employee HSA contribution made through a payroll deduction is still wages for employment-tax purposes;
- an HSA salary reduction made under a **section 125 cafeteria plan** is not wages and is not subject to employment taxes or withholding.

That means the extra payroll advantage exists only when the employer's HSA contribution arrangement qualifies for that section 125 treatment.

For the 2026 example, the IRS lists the employee Social Security rate as 6.2% and the employee Medicare rate as 1.45%.

## The formula

    extra employee payroll-tax saving
    = eligible section 125 HSA salary reduction × (6.2% + 1.45%)

This isolates the payroll-tax advantage. It does **not** try to guess your federal income-tax bracket, state tax, Additional Medicare Tax, or whether part of your wages is above the Social Security wage base.

## Worked example

Use the official 2026 self-only HSA limit and assume the full contribution is made through an eligible section 125 salary-reduction arrangement, with all of that pay otherwise subject to both employee Social Security and Medicare tax.

    2026 self-only HSA contribution = $4,400
    employee Social Security rate    = 6.2%
    employee Medicare rate           = 1.45%
    combined employee rate           = 7.65%

    extra payroll-tax saving
    = $4,400 × 7.65%
    = $336.60

So the payroll route is worth **$336.60 more** than a direct HSA contribution in this narrow 2026 example, before considering federal income-tax deductions that can apply to HSA contributions through either qualifying route.

The same calculation should not simply be copied into 2027: this page has verified the 2027 HSA limits, but the worked payroll-tax rates above are specifically the official **2026** employment-tax rates.

## What would flip the answer

- **Your payroll deduction is not a section 125 salary reduction.** Then the special employment-tax exclusion used in the example does not apply.
- **You are not HSA-eligible for the full year.** Your allowable contribution can be lower than the headline annual limit.
- **You enroll in Medicare.** IRS Publication 969 says the contribution limit becomes zero beginning with the first month of Medicare enrollment.
- **Your HDHP does not meet the year's HSA rules.** The minimum deductible rises from $1,700/$3,400 in 2026 to $1,750/$3,500 in 2027, so the plan itself has to qualify before the contribution limit matters.
