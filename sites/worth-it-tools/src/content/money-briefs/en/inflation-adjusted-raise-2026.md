  - q: "Is a raise equal to inflation really nothing?"
    a: "In purchasing power, yes — it holds you exactly level, which is what the arithmetic says. It is worth naming, because a matching raise is usually announced as a raise and received as one. ## The formula, and why subtraction is not it

    real raise = ((1 + nominal raise) ÷ (1 + inflation)) − 1

Both a raise and a price increase apply to a base, so they compound rather than add. Subtracting is an approximation that is close enough at small numbers and drifts as they grow:

| Nominal raise | Inflation | Subtraction says | Correct real raise |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 3.0% | 2.8% | 0.20% | **0.19%** |
| 5.0% | 2.8% | 2.20% | **2.14%** |
| 2.5% | 2.8% | −0.30% | **−0.29%** |
| 12.0% | 9.0% | 3.00% | **2.75%** |
| 0.0% | 2.8% | −2.80% | **−2.72%** |

## What it is in dollars

Against the 2.8% cost-of-living adjustment set for 2026, on a $70,000 salary:

| Nominal raise | New salary | Real value in prior-year dollars | Real gain |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0% | $70,000 | $68,093 | **−$1,907** |
| 2.0% | $71,400 | $69,455 | **−$545** |
| 2.8% | $71,960 | $70,000 | **$0** |
| 3.0% | $72,100 | $70,136 | **+$136** |
| 4.5% | $73,150 | $71,157 | **+$1,157** |
| 6.0% | $74,200 | $72,179 | **+$2,179** |

The row worth sitting with is the first. A year with no raise is not a neutral year — it is a $1,907 pay cut, delivered without a conversation and without appearing on any document.

The second row worth sitting with is the 3% one. A $2,100 increase produces $136 of actual gain. Of the raise, 94% went to standing still.

## Recovering lost ground

Real losses do not reverse on their own. If you took less than inflation last year, this year's raise has to cover both years:

    required raise = ((1 + infl₁) × (1 + infl₂) ÷ (1 + raise₁)) − 1

Someone who took 1.5% last year against 2.8%, facing 2.8% again:

    (1.028 × 1.028 ÷ 1.015) − 1 = 4.12%

So the ask this year is 4.12% simply to be where you were two years ago — and a 3% offer, which sounds unremarkable, leaves the gap open. This is the arithmetic behind the common experience of a decade of raises that never quite arrives anywhere.

## What would reverse the conclusion

- **Your personal inflation rate is lower.** A fixed-rate mortgage, no childcare and a stable commute can put a household well below the index. National figures are averages of budgets, not of people.
- **Non-salary compensation moved.** A larger employer retirement contribution, a lower health premium, or added leave can be worth more than a nominal increase, and none of it shows in the salary line.
- **The raise came with a promotion.** Then the comparison is not this job's pay against last year's, it is this role's pay against the market for it. A 3% real-zero increase attached to a different job is a different question.
- **A threshold effect.** Crossing a bracket boundary, a benefit cliff or a contribution ceiling changes the net result in either direction, and on a modest raise the threshold can be larger than the raise.
