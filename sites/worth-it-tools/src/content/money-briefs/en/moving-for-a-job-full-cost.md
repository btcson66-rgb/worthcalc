## The two kinds of cost

A relocation has a one-off cost that feels enormous and a recurring cost that feels invisible. The recurring one decides the answer.

    annual net gain = raise − housing difference × 12 − tax difference − commuting difference
    break-even months = one-off moving cost ÷ (annual net gain ÷ 12)

## The arithmetic

Worked example — a $12,000 raise to $85,000, moving to a market with higher rent and a state income tax 2 points higher:

| Line | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Salary increase | +$12,000 |
| Housing difference, $450 a month | −$5,400 |
| State income tax, 2% of $85,000 | −$1,700 |
| **Recurring annual net gain** | **+$4,900** |

| Year | Cash position |
| --- | ---: |
| Year 1 (net gain less a $6,500 move) | **−$1,600** |
| Year 2 | +$4,900 |
| Year 3 | +$4,900 |

    break-even = $6,500 ÷ ($4,900 ÷ 12) = 15.9 months

A $12,000 raise turned into $4,900, and year one is a loss. Neither of those is a reason not to move — but both are usually a surprise, because the offer was evaluated against the old salary rather than against the new cost of living.

## Where the money actually goes

Of the $12,000:

- **$5,400** to housing. Almost always the largest term, and the one people check least rigorously. Use real listings for the neighbourhood you would genuinely live in.
- **$1,700** to state income tax. Check the full picture: a state with no income tax usually collects through property or sales tax instead.
- **$6,500** once, to the move itself. Recovered by month 16 and then irrelevant.

## The trap in the other direction

Moving to a *cheaper* market for a lower salary has the same structure and a worse ending. Housing, food and services scale with local prices; **savings, retirement contributions and existing debt do not.** A 20% pay cut into a 20% cheaper city leaves spending roughly level and saving materially worse, because the dollars going into a retirement account are the same dollars everywhere.

## What would reverse the conclusion

- **A relocation package.** Net it for tax and read the clawback period. A $10,000 package at 22% is about $7,800, and it becomes a debt if you leave inside the stated window.
- **A shorter or removed commute.** If the new role is remote or nearby, the commuting line can be worth thousands a year in cash and hundreds of hours in time — often more than the housing difference.
- **A second move you can already see.** If the role is a two-year step and another relocation follows, count both moves against the gain, which can push break-even past the time you will stay.
- **Career depth rather than salary.** A market with more employers in your field is worth something the year you need a new job, and that value does not appear in any of the lines above.
