## Both paths, in the same unit

Comparing $42 an hour of overtime with a $30-an-hour second job compares two numbers that are not measuring the same thing. One is paid from the minute you stay at your desk; the other starts after a journey and some unpaid preparation. And for 2025–2028 the two are no longer taxed alike either.

    net hourly = (gross hourly × paid hours × (1 − marginal rate)
                  + qualified overtime premium × federal marginal rate
                  − travel cost)
                 ÷ (paid hours + unpaid hours attached to the shift)

## The 2025–2028 overtime deduction

| Rule (IRS) | Figure |
| --- | ---: |
| Tax years covered | **2025–2028** |
| What counts | the FLSA-required premium above the regular rate — the **0.5×** in time-and-a-half |
| Annual maximum deduction | **$12,500** ($25,000 on a joint return) |
| Phase-out begins at MAGI of | **$150,000** ($300,000 joint) |
| Phase-out rate | **$100 per $1,000** of MAGI above the threshold |

It reduces federal income tax only. Social Security, Medicare and most state taxes still apply to the whole overtime cheque, and second-job wages get nothing comparable.

## Worked example

A $28 base wage, a six-hour block, a 27% combined marginal rate of which 22% is federal income tax, and a taxpayer well under both the cap and the phase-out:

| | Overtime at 1.5× | Second job at $30 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Gross for the block | $252.00 | $180.00 |
| Tax at 27% | −$68.04 | −$48.60 |
| Overtime deduction: $84 premium × 22% | +$18.48 | — |
| Travel, both ways | — | −$9.00 |
| **Net received** | **$202.44** | **$122.40** |
| Paid hours | 6.0 | 6.0 |
| Unpaid hours attached | 0.0 | 1.6 |
| **Net per hour committed** | **$33.74** | **$16.11** |

The premium is $28 × 0.5 × 6 = $84, and at a 22% federal rate the deduction is worth $18.48 on this block. The advertised gap was $42 against $30 — a 40% difference. The real gap is $33.74 against $16.11, about 2.1 to 1. Most of that widening comes from the unpaid hours; the deduction adds the rest.

## What the second job has to pay to draw level

Solve for the wage that equalises net per hour committed:

    required gross hourly = (overtime net per committed hour × total hours committed + travel) ÷ (paid hours × (1 − marginal rate))

At the figures above, that is about **$60.60 an hour** — roughly 2.16× the $28 base wage. Across different travel and preparation burdens:

| Unpaid hours per 6h shift | Travel cost | Wage needed to match overtime | Multiple of base |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0.0 (fully remote) | $0 | $46.22 | 1.65× |
| 0.8 | $5 | $53.52 | 1.91× |
| 1.6 | $9 | $60.60 | 2.16× |
| 2.5 | $14 | $68.67 | 2.45× |

Read the first row. Before 2025 a fully remote second job only had to match the overtime rate itself, 1.5×. The deduction lifts that floor to about 1.65×, because the overtime premium now keeps more of each dollar after federal tax.

## The availability problem

All of this assumes the overtime exists. In practice it is rationed by the employer, which turns the question into a different one:

    expected value = (hours actually offered × overtime net rate) + (remaining hours × second-job net rate)

Six hours of overtime you can reliably get is worth $202.44. Twelve hours of second-job time, across two shifts, is worth $244.80. So the second job can still win on **total** while losing badly per hour — and that is usually the real trade: not which rate is better, but whether enough of the better-paid hours are available to fill the need.

## What would reverse the conclusion

- **Remote or on-site-free second work.** Removing travel and preparation removes most of the advantage, leaving the 1.5× premium and the deduction to overcome.
- **A genuine skill premium.** Work that pays well over 2× your base rate because it is skilled, licensed or unsociable clears the bar on the rate alone.
- **Overtime you cannot get, or that isn't FLSA overtime.** A rate you are not offered is not a rate, and premiums an employer pays beyond what the FLSA requires do not qualify for the deduction.
- **High income.** Above $150,000 of MAGI ($300,000 joint) the deduction shrinks by $100 per $1,000 and eventually disappears, taking the tax edge with it.
- **Something that compounds.** A second job that builds a client base, a portfolio or a qualification is not being bought for its hourly rate, and judging it by that rate answers the wrong question.
