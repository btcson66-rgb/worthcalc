## Both paths, in the same unit

Comparing $42 an hour of overtime with a $30-an-hour second job compares two numbers that are not measuring the same thing. One is paid from the minute you stay at your desk; the other starts after a journey and some unpaid preparation.

    net hourly = (gross hourly × paid hours × (1 − marginal rate) − travel cost)
                 ÷ (paid hours + unpaid hours attached to the shift)

Worked, at a $28 base wage and a 27% combined marginal rate, over a six-hour block:

| | Overtime at 1.5× | Second job at $30 |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Gross for the block | $252 | $180 |
| Tax at 27% | −$68 | −$49 |
| Travel, both ways | — | −$9 |
| **Net received** | **$184** | **$122** |
| Paid hours | 6.0 | 6.0 |
| Unpaid hours attached | 0.0 | 1.6 |
| **Net per hour committed** | **$30.66** | **$16.11** |

The advertised gap was $42 against $30 — a 40% difference. The real gap is $30.66 against $16.11, which is 90%. Two thirds of that widening comes from the unpaid hours, not from the pay rate.

## What the second job has to pay to draw level

Solve for the wage that equalises net per hour committed:

    required gross hourly = (overtime net per committed hour × total hours committed + travel) ÷ (paid hours × (1 − marginal rate))

At the figures above, that is about **$55 an hour** — roughly 1.97× the $28 base wage. Across different travel and preparation burdens:

| Unpaid hours per 6h shift | Travel cost | Wage needed to match overtime | Multiple of base |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0.0 (fully remote) | $0 | $42.00 | 1.50× |
| 0.8 | $5 | $48.74 | 1.74× |
| 1.6 | $9 | $55.25 | 1.97× |
| 2.5 | $14 | $62.70 | 2.24× |

Read the first row. A remote second job with no travel and no unpaid time needs only to match the overtime rate itself — which is why remote second work is a genuinely different proposition from a shift somewhere else, rather than a slightly better version of it.

## The availability problem

All of this assumes the overtime exists. In practice it is rationed by the employer, which turns the question into a different one:

    expected value = (hours actually offered × overtime net rate) + (remaining hours × second-job net rate)

Six hours of overtime you can reliably get is worth $184. Twelve hours of second-job time, across two shifts, is worth $245. So the second job wins on **total**, while losing badly per hour — and that is usually the real trade: not which rate is better, but whether enough of the better-paid hours are available to fill the need.

## What would reverse the conclusion

- **Remote or on-site-free second work.** Removing travel and preparation removes the entire advantage, leaving only the 1.5× premium to overcome.
- **A genuine skill premium.** Work that pays 2× your base rate because it is skilled, licensed or unsociable clears the bar on the rate alone, without needing any of the friction arguments.
- **Overtime you cannot get.** A rate you are not offered is not a rate. The comparison only decides where to put the hours you have a choice about.
- **Something that compounds.** A second job that builds a client base, a portfolio or a qualification is not being bought for its hourly rate, and judging it by that rate answers the wrong question.
