## The decision is not about probability

Most advice on this treats it as an expected-value bet: damage is unlikely, the waiver is expensive, decline it. That reasoning is incomplete, because what you are exposed to when you decline depends entirely on what pays instead.

    cost of declining = P(damage) × (deductible + total surcharge + loss-of-use and fees)

Three fallback positions produce three very different numbers.

## The three positions

**Primary cover.** A card that pays first, without involving your own insurer. A claim costs you nothing and leaves no mark on your auto record.

| | |
| --- | ---: |
| Waiver price, 7 days at $32 | $224 |
| Exposure if you decline | ≈ $0 |

Declining is clearly right. The waiver buys nothing you do not already have.

**Secondary cover.** The card pays only what your own insurer does not — so a claim runs through your personal policy first.

| Item | Amount |
| --- | ---: |
| Your collision deductible | $1,000 |
| Premium surcharge, $460 a year for 3 years | $1,380 |
| **Exposure per damaging incident** | **$2,380** |

At a 3% chance of damage on a given rental, that is an expected cost of about $71 against a $224 waiver — so declining still wins on average. But you are now carrying a $2,380 tail to save $224, and the card's "cover" pays after the part that actually hurt you.

**No cover at all.** No personal auto policy and no card benefit. The exposure is the full repair cost plus loss-of-use, and the waiver is usually the right purchase.

## What the counter will not tell you

Three charges sit outside most people's mental model of "damage":

- **Loss of use** — the rental firm's lost revenue while the car is repaired, sometimes billed at the daily rate for the whole repair period.
- **Diminished value** — the claimed drop in the vehicle's resale value.
- **Administrative fees** — a flat charge for processing the claim.

A waiver typically disposes of all three. Card and personal-policy cover vary on whether they pay them, and they are the most commonly disputed part of a rental claim.

## What would reverse the conclusion

- **An exclusion you did not check.** Vehicle class, country, and rental length are the usual three. A card benefit that excludes vans, or rentals over 15 days, or the country you are in, is worth zero on that trip.
- **No personal auto policy.** Secondary cover behind nothing may pay nothing. This is the most common gap for people who do not own a car.
- **A long rental.** The waiver scales with days while the exposure does not, so a 30-day rental at $32 a day is $960 against the same $2,380 tail. Long rentals push toward declining even from a weaker position.
- **A trip where a dispute would be ruinous.** If a two-week argument over a repair estimate would wreck the trip or the work it was for, buying the waiver is a purchase of certainty. Call it that rather than pretending it is good insurance.
