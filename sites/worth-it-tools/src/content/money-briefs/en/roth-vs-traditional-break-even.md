## The comparison, stated properly

    traditional after-tax value = contribution × (1 + r)^n × (1 − future rate)
    Roth after-tax value        = contribution × (1 + r)^n

Multiplication commutes, so paying tax at the start and paying it at the end produce the same result **when the rate is the same**. That is the whole standard answer, and it is correct:

| | Traditional | Roth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Future rate **lower** than today's | **Wins** | |
| Future rate **higher** than today's | | **Wins** |
| Rates equal | Tie | Tie |

Everything else people argue about is a forecast of your own income decades out, under tax law that will change. Which is why the tie-breaker matters.

## The tie-breaker: the cap is a nominal number

The 2026 elective deferral limit is $24,500 for both. But $24,500 of traditional and $24,500 of Roth cost you different amounts of actual money.

At a 22% marginal rate:

| | Contribution | Cost in take-home pay | Sheltered |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Traditional | $24,500 | **$19,110** | $24,500 pre-tax |
| Roth | $24,500 | **$24,500** | $24,500 post-tax |

A traditional contribution at the cap moves $19,110 of your money inside the shelter. A Roth contribution at the cap moves $24,500. That is **28% more of your own money** under the same nominal limit.

This only bites if you are actually contributing the maximum. Below the cap you can equalise by contributing more to the traditional account, and the tie returns.

## What the numbers look like

$24,500 contributed, 20 years at 7%, withdrawn at a 22% rate:

| | Traditional | Roth |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Value after 20 years | $94,808 | $94,808 |
| Tax on withdrawal | −$20,858 | $0 |
| **After-tax** | **$73,950** | **$94,808** |
| Cost in take-home today | $19,110 | $24,500 |

The Roth ends 28% higher and cost 28% more. At equal rates that is a wash — exactly as the formula says. The asymmetry appears only when the cap stops you contributing the extra.

## The practical ranking

1. **Capture the employer match** in whatever account the plan puts it in. An immediate 50–100% return outranks this entire question.
2. **Early career, low bracket** → Roth. Your rate is unlikely to be lower later, and paying tax now at a low rate is cheap.
3. **Peak earning years, high bracket** → traditional usually wins, because the deduction is worth the most exactly when your rate is highest.
4. **Maxing out either way** → the limit asymmetry tilts to Roth.
5. **Genuinely unsure** → split. Holding both lets you choose which account to draw from each year in retirement, and that flexibility is worth something on its own.

## What would reverse the conclusion

- **A large expected rate drop.** Someone at 32% today withdrawing at 12% should take the deduction, and no limit asymmetry closes a 20-point gap.
- **State taxes.** Moving from a high-tax state to a no-income-tax one in retirement makes the deduction more valuable than the federal comparison alone suggests.
- **Required withdrawals.** Traditional balances are subject to required minimum distributions that can force income you do not need; Roth balances have historically been treated differently. That is a planning difference, not a rate difference.
- **Rules that change.** Contribution and catch-up treatment for higher earners has been amended repeatedly. Check the current position before committing to a multi-year plan.
