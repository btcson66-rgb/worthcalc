## The two figures

| | 2026 |
| --- | ---: |
| Social Security cost-of-living adjustment | **2.8%** |
| Standard Medicare Part B premium | **$202.90/month** |
| Increase in that premium | **+$17.90** (≈10%) |

One is a percentage applied to your benefit. The other is a flat dollar amount taken out of it. That difference is the whole story.

## The arithmetic

    net monthly gain = (benefit × COLA) − premium increase

| Monthly benefit | COLA at 2.8% | Less Part B rise | Net gain | Share consumed |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| $1,200 | $33.60 | −$17.90 | **$15.70** | 53% |
| $1,600 | $44.80 | −$17.90 | **$26.90** | 40% |
| $2,000 | $56.00 | −$17.90 | **$38.10** | **32%** |
| $3,000 | $84.00 | −$17.90 | **$66.10** | 21% |

The same COLA and the same premium produce very different outcomes, because one term scales with the benefit and the other does not.

## Where the raise disappears entirely

    benefit at which the raise is fully consumed = premium increase ÷ COLA rate
                                                 = $17.90 ÷ 0.028
                                                 = about $639 a month

Below roughly $639 of monthly benefit, the Part B increase is larger than the COLA. The hold-harmless provision generally stops the net benefit from actually falling for people in that position — but it stops the number going *down*, it does not hand back the raise.

## Why this is not a one-off

The structure repeats every year: the COLA is a percentage, the premium increase is a flat amount, and medical costs have generally risen faster than the general index the COLA tracks. A household whose spending is concentrated in medical care and housing can receive the full COLA every year and still lose purchasing power every year.

That is not an argument about whether the COLA is calculated correctly. It is an argument for budgeting on your own net number rather than on the headline.

## What to do with it

1. **Take your own benefit**, not an average, from your SSA statement.
2. **Multiply by 2.8%** to get your gross raise.
3. **Subtract your own Part B change.** If you pay an income-related amount, the deduction is larger than $17.90 and you need your own figure.
4. **Use the result** in the budget. For a mid-range benefit it is roughly two-thirds of what the headline implied.

## What would reverse the conclusion

- **A benefit well above average.** At $3,000 a month the premium takes 21% of the raise rather than 53%, and the net gain is real money.
- **Not being enrolled in Part B.** Someone covered through an employer plan or otherwise not paying the premium keeps the full 2.8%.
- **An income-related premium adjustment.** Higher-income beneficiaries pay above the standard premium, so their deduction — and the share of the raise it consumes — is larger than anything in the table above.
