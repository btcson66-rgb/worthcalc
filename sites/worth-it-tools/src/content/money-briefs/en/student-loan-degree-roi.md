## The cost nobody invoices

Tuition and loans arrive with paperwork. The larger cost does not.

Worked example — four years of study, $27,000 borrowed at 6.52% and repaid over ten years, against $35,000 a year that could have been earned instead:

| Component | Amount | Share |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Total loan repayment | $42,824 | 23% |
| Forgone earnings, 4 × $35,000 | **$140,000** | **77%** |
| **Total investment** | **$182,824** | |

The debt everybody argues about is under a quarter of the cost. The three-quarters that nobody invoices is the time.

## The payback

    payback years = total investment ÷ annual earnings premium

| Annual earnings premium | Payback after graduation |
| ---: | ---: |
| $15,000 | 12.2 years |
| **$25,000** | **7.3 years** |
| $40,000 | 4.6 years |
| $8,000 | 22.9 years |

Everything in that table is the same degree with the same debt. What moves is the premium — and the premium is a property of the *field*, not of the credential.

## Why the median is the wrong number

Published earnings-by-attainment figures are medians across very large populations. They are true and they are the wrong resolution for a personal decision, because the distribution behind them is wide enough that the same qualification is an excellent investment in one field and a poor one in another.

Use the narrowest data you can find: the field, and where it is published, the institution. A national median tells you what happened to a population, not what is likely to happen to you.

## The two levers that actually move it

**Reduce forgone earnings.** This is the largest term, so it is the most responsive. Earning $15,000 a year while studying cuts that line from $140,000 to $80,000 — total investment falls 33%, which no tuition discount is likely to match.

**Finish.** Non-completion is the scenario that inverts the whole calculation: the debt and the forgone earnings are incurred, and most of the premium is not. Multiplying the premium by a realistic completion probability is the single most important correction an honest version of this arithmetic makes.

## What would reverse the conclusion

- **A licensed profession.** Where the credential is a legal requirement to practise, the alternative is not a lower salary — it is the job not existing. The payback calculation does not describe that choice.
- **Employer funding.** Tuition paid by an employer and study part-time around work removes both large terms at once, and the arithmetic stops being close.
- **A field with a small premium.** At an $8,000 premium the payback is 23 years, which for most people is not an investment case at all, whatever else the degree is worth.
- **Everything the money does not measure.** Unemployment rates fall with attainment, and stability has value this page does not price.
