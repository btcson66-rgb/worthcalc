## The comparison people run, and the one that decides it

The quote sheet shows two premiums for the same death benefit:

| | Monthly premium |
| --- | ---: |
| 30-year level term | **$40** |
| Whole life | **$400** |

Read that way, term is ten times cheaper and the conversation ends. But the cheaper policy leaves $360 a month over, and what happens to that money is the whole question.

    value of buying term = (whole life premium − term premium), compounded

| Difference invested at | 30-year value |
| --- | ---: |
| 4% | $249,700 |
| **6%** | **$361,600** |
| 8% | $536,800 |

Whole life has to produce more than that — in cash value, plus the value of cover continuing after year 30 — to have been the better purchase.

## Why the early years matter so much

Whole life costs are front-loaded. Cash value in the first several years is commonly a small fraction of premiums paid, and can be zero.

That has two consequences:

1. **An early exit is the worst outcome.** Surrendering in year five typically returns less than was paid in, having also cost the compounding the term-plus-investing route would have had.
2. **The comparison needs a long hold to be fair.** Judging whole life at year five judges it at its worst point by design. Judging it at year thirty is the honest test — and year thirty is where the $361,600 is waiting on the other side.

## The argument that actually favours permanent cover

It is not the return. It is behaviour.

"Buy term and invest the difference" is arithmetic that assumes the difference gets invested. For a large share of people it does not — it gets absorbed into ordinary spending, at which point its compounded value is zero and whole life wins by default.

A permanent policy is a forced savings mechanism with an insurance wrapper. That is a real feature with a real cost, and the honest way to price it is: what is the probability you would actually invest $360 every month for thirty years without being billed for it?

## When the rate comparison does not apply at all

Some needs do not expire, and term cannot meet them at any price:

- **A dependant who will need support for life**, where cover must outlast any term.
- **Estate liquidity**, where tax is due and the assets are illiquid.
- **A business buy-sell agreement** funded by a policy on a partner.

In those cases the question is not which is cheaper. It is which product solves the problem.

## What would reverse the conclusion

- **A realistic expectation that the difference is spent.** Then term's advantage is arithmetic that never happens.
- **A guaranteed return you value above an expected one.** A contractual 4% and a hoped-for 6% carry different risk; someone who would not tolerate the variance is not being irrational by paying for the guarantee.
- **Health that makes term renewal impossible.** Cover bought young and permanently is cover that cannot be underwritten away later.
- **A very long horizon.** The longer the hold, the more the front-loaded costs amortise and the closer permanent cover gets to the invested difference.
